\chapter{Summary and Conclusions}

\paragraph{Summary.}
This treatise has presented a comprehensive, unified framework for the modeling and tracking control of general rigid body systems.
In contrast to established methods that rely on minimal coordinates, this framework allows redundant configuration coordinates and nonholonomic velocity coordinates.
This is crucial for the derivation of equations of motion (modeling) and the formulation of desired closed-loop dynamics (control design) in a global and singularity-free manner.

Using insights from the modeling of a generic rigid body system, the proposed control design defines a template for the desired closed-loop dynamics that shares the model geometry and kinematics while having generic kinetic parameters that serve as tuning values.
These are the established inertia parameters---total mass, center of mass, and moment of inertia---as well as the analogous, though not yet established, parameters for damping and stiffness.
While suitable closed-loop dynamics for a static reference are evident, natural closed-loop dynamics for tracking an accelerated reference trajectory are not.
This work presented three possible formulations, motivated by a particle distribution, the individual rigid bodies, and the total error energy.
For fully actuated systems, the desired closed-loop dynamics can be realized exactly.
For the case of underactuated systems or the presence of input inequality constraints, a static optimization approach inspired by Gauss' principle of least constraint still leads to an explicit control law.

The proposed modeling and control approach is demonstrated and discussed using academic examples such as a prismatic joint, a rotary joint, or the PVTOL.
However, the true benefit of a systematic framework becomes apparent for larger systems whose equations of motion are far too cumbersome to be analyzed individually.
The examples of a 6-DoF robotic arm and a bicopter concept were discussed through simulation results.
Finally, the practical feasibility was demonstrated through flight tests with the custom-developed tricopter and quadcopter while tracking aerobatic maneuvers.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
\fixme{
    The primary contributions of this research span across mathematical extensions, theoretical mechanics formalisms, control design synthesis, and experimental validation:
}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Coordinates in mechanics:}
The established literature on analytical mechanics is somewhat split: textbooks focused on engineering address formulations for general rigid body systems but restrict themselves to classical calculus with minimal generalized coordinates, e.g.\ \cite{Schwertassek:MultibodySystems}.
On the other hand, texts with a physics focus utilize modern methods from Lie group theory to derive natural formulations, but are usually only applied to small academic examples, e.g.\ \cite{Marsden:MechanicsAndSymmetry}.
The second and third chapters of this work are an attempt to combine these by formulating the central tools of analytical mechanics in a form that allows for a natural parameterization while requiring only elementary calculus in their application.

Many of these tools, such as the directional derivative, the Hessian matrix, or the calculus of variations, also find application in other fields of physics and mathematics.

\item \textbf{Rigid body damping and stiffness:}
While inertia is arguably the most crucial part of kinetics, this work investigated damping and stiffness with equal focus.
It was shown that any constellation of linear springs between two rigid bodies can be represented by a potential with 10 parameters that are defined completely analogously to the established inertia parameters.
From the point of view of this text, if the very established formulation of the rigid body kinetic energy in terms of total mass, center of mass, and moment of inertia is standard, then the proposed formulation of the stiffness potential in terms of total stiffness, center of stiffness, and moment of stiffness should be equally standard.

The rigid body potential can generally be useful as a natural cost function for optimization problems over $\SpecialEuclideanGroup(3)$, e.g.\ in an extension of Wahba's problem that is omnipresent in navigation.

\item \textbf{Rigid body system formalism:}
Application of the extended tools for analytical mechanics and the modeling of inertia, gravitation, damping, and stiffness leads to a unified framework for the derivation of equations of motion for general rigid body systems.
The final section of the third chapter presents an implementation-ready recipe.
In contrast to established formalisms (e.g.\ Denavit-Hartenberg), it allows for redundant configuration coordinates and minimal nonholonomic velocity coordinates.
Furthermore, there is no restriction on the choice of body-fixed frames.

\item \textbf{Rigid body system closed-loop template.}
The fourth chapter proposed three slightly different templates for desired closed-loop dynamics.
The first two are completely determined by the rigid body structure of the model, so for application we only need to assign reasonable values to the constitutive parameters to achieve the desired tuning.
The definitions of the closed-loop dynamics are completely invariant to the choice of coordinates; i.e., the closed-loop dynamics are covariant.
The only need for a choice of coordinates is for practical implementation.

One may also regard the results of this part as one possible answer to the question: ``What is a natural, asymptotically stable, second-order dynamics on the given configuration manifold $\configSpace \subseteq (\SpecialEuclideanGroup(3))^\numRigidBodies$?''

\item \textbf{Control of underactuated systems.}
For the case of underactuated systems and/or input constraints, this work proposed an optimization scheme for the system acceleration to be as close as possible to the acceleration computed by the closed-loop template in the sense of minimizing the Gaussian constraint.
Though no general proof of stability was found for this approach, simulation results with the PVTOL and the bicopter, as well as the actual flight tests with the quadcopter, show the feasibility of this approach.
For each of these cases, it was crucial to allow for inertial and stiffness cross-couplings, parameterized by distinct values for the center of mass and center of stiffness, as introduced above in the rigid body potential.

\item \textbf{Multicopter realization.}
While the focus of this treatise is clearly the theory of rigid body control, the experimental realization of the tricopter and quadcopter is equally challenging.
The fifth chapter shows just an excerpt of the many little topics that have to be addressed until they may be used as a test bench for rigid body dynamics.

\end{itemize}
