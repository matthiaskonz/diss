\paragraph{Mechanical frame.}
The mechanical design of the LSR-quad and tricopter (see \autoref{fig:LSRQuadAndTri}) is kept as simple as possible.
A central body holds the electronic parts, including the battery.
It is a sandwich of two water-jet-cut carbon-fiber plates that clamp together the internal 3D-printed parts.
These in turn clamp the carbon-fiber tubes (the arms) or, in the case of the tricopter, its bearings (see \autoref{fig:TriInnereien}).
From there, carbon-fiber tubes stretch out to hold the propeller motors.
The propellers are directly bolted to their motors.
The radius at which the propellers are attached is intentionally the same for quad and tricopter, $\ArmRadius = 240\,\unit{mm}$.
The outer, flexible carbon-fiber rings serve as collision protection and landing skid.

\paragraph{Propeller drive.}
The quadcopter and tricopter share the same $10'' = 254\,\unit{mm}$ propellers, brushless DC motors, and driver electronics.
The driver electronics and firmware are also LSR developments, but they will not be discussed further in this work.
Overall, the drive allows for angular velocities up to $120\,\unit{Hz}$, corresponding to a maximum thrust of about $8\,\unit{N}$.
More crucially, it allows for very fast response times including active braking by feeding current back into the battery.

For the tricopter, the tilting mechanism is driven by a hobbyist servo that is connected to a gear on the arm by a toothed belt (see \autoref{fig:TriInnereien}) and allows for tilt angles of $\pm 75^\circ$.

\paragraph{Sensors.}
The quadcopter and tricopter utilize the same sensors.
An inertial measurement unit (IMU) VN100s from \textsc{VectorNav} is mounted on the central body to measure angular velocity and inertial acceleration.
It also contains an internal algorithm for bias and attitude estimation, which is used for outdoor experiments that are not the subject of this work.

Inside the LSR lab, a camera-based motion-capture system from \textsc{Vicon} measures position and attitude via reflective markers on the multicopters.
The motion-capture software runs on the ground-station Windows 7 PC, and the measurements are transferred to the multicopter processor via a wireless link.
Overall, this results in a low sampling rate and significant latency in these measurements.
The algorithm used to fuse the high bandwidth IMU measurements with the Vicon measurements will be presented in the following section.

\paragraph{User interface.}
A \textsc{Futaba} two-joystick remote control with several additional analog and digital inputs is used as a reliable and near real-time user interface.
It is used for manual piloting the multicopters as well as realizing security mechanisms like triggering emergency landing or even propeller shutdown.

The ground station computer runs a graphical user interface (GUI) implemented in \textsc{Matlab}.
It displays and may record real-time data and can issue discrete commands to the multicopter controller.
Furthermore, this software also handles the forwarding of the Vicon data.
The communication link is realized by two XBee S6B Wi-Fi modules.

\paragraph{Mainboard \& processor.}
The center of the onboard electronics is a custom-built mainboard.
It transforms and distributes the battery power to the other components and connects the data lines of the sensors and actuators to the main processor:
an Atmel AT32UC3C 32-bit, 66~\unit{MHz} microcontroller with floating-point-unit (FPU).

\paragraph{Firmware.}
The firmware on the main processor does not utilize an operating system but is programmed directly using C \& C++ code compiled by \texttt{avr32-gcc}.
It uses the Atmel software framework as a low-level interface to the microcontroller peripherals.
At the top level, the template library \textsc{Eigen} offers linear-algebra objects and routines for the rigid-body controller.
A rough overview of the microcontroller firmware is given in \autoref{fig:MicocontrollerCodeStructure}.

\paragraph{Multicopter agent.}
The critical control part of the multicopter firmware, here called the \textit{multicopter agent}, is also used in a \textsc{Simulink} simulation of the closed loop with a multicopter model.
To be precise, the same C++ source files are included in both the firmware compilation and the compilation of the \textsc{Simulink} block.

The multicopter model, the estimation of its parameters, and experimental validation will be discussed in the next section.
For this work, the feedback controller for the quadcopter and tricopter is of paramount interest.
However, its realization depends on accurate state estimation, realization of the control forces, and generation of feasible reference motion.
These are also parts of the multicopter agent, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:StructureMulticopterAgent}, and will be the subject of the next sections.
A coarse overview of this is given in \cite{Konz:Mechatronics} and its book version \cite{Konz:MechatronicsBook}.
